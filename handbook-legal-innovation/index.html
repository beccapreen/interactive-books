Chapter 1
The Impetus for Change
Why change, and why now? The market forces making innovation non-optional.
Clients now expect innovation
+
91% of corporate clients surveyed consider it important that panel law firms leverage innovative technology. RFPs now routinely ask about technology use, service delivery models, and digital transformation capabilities. Firms that can't answer these questions credibly risk losing panel positions.
ALSPs are capturing market share
+
Alternative Legal Service Providers grew from $8.4bn in 2015 to over $14bn. 79% of US law firms and 72% of US corporations now use ALSPs. One large firm leader admitted ALSPs had stolen 8% of annual revenue for two consecutive years.
In-house teams are keeping more work
+
Corporate legal departments are increasingly sophisticated, using technology and legal operations (CLOC) to handle work in-house. This means less work goes to outside counsel, so firms must fight harder for a shrinking pie using new tools and approaches.
LegalTech investment is booming
+
LegalTech investment topped $41bn by mid-2021, a five-fold increase from 2009. There are now over 2,000 legal technology solutions globally. The market is rich with tools for automation, AI, analytics, and more.
Structural barriers remain the real challenge
+
Money allocated to innovation teams means little if structural barriers aren't addressed. Without changing how legal work is defined, accepting non-lawyer professionals, and being willing to explore new billing models, leading change is an uphill battle requiring stamina, resilience, vision, and strategy.

Chapter 2
Establishing a Vision
Start with "Why" — define your innovation function's purpose before developing strategy.
Sinek's Golden Circle: Why → How → What
+
Companies that lead with Why inspire people. Your innovation team's "Why" should be about transformation — not a list of projects. Example: "We help transform the practice of law to make it better — for clients, for consumers of legal services, and for those who work in the industry."
Vision should be durable, not tied to specific actions
+
If your mission references specific actions (like "streamline documents"), it becomes obsolete as the landscape shifts. A purpose-driven vision allows you to evolve. Apple's vision about "challenging the status quo" works across any product line — your legal innovation vision should do the same.
Get your team involved in defining the vision
+
Speak to firm management and invested partners to understand why they believe an innovation function matters. Then ask your team what drives them — find the common thread. Involvement from the beginning means you won't face poor adoption later.

Chapter 3
Innovation Strategies for Culture Change
The Three Es — the book's core strategic framework.
Success = Adoption, not rollout. Rolling something out doesn't mean innovation has happened. People must actually use it.
Education — Build urgency and awareness
+
The critical first step. Most firms dramatically underestimate the importance of education. Key mechanisms:
  • Firm-wide newsletters on market changes affecting clients
  • CLE series featuring client legal ops voices
  • Summer/fall associate programming on the business of law
  • "How to speak to clients about..." training for partners
Don't assume young lawyers are tech-savvy. Ubiquitous personal tech can actually stifle interest in workplace tools.
Engagement — Involve people in solutions
+
The best way to drive adoption is to involve end users in developing, configuring, and implementing solutions. Key tactics:
  • Involve end users during technology demos
  • Run pilots and POCs with engaged practitioners
  • Bake innovation contribution into performance reviews
  • Create internal "certifications" for innovation milestones
Deciding to invest in technology without engaging lawyers is a sure-fire way to end up with a graveyard of unused tools.
Execution — Get things done and prove value
+
Execution is more than implementation — it means rolling out successfully, communicating wins, managing change, and tracking adoption. The three Es work as iterative cycles that amplify each time they revolve. Educational programming circulates news of executed projects, which generates engagement for the next cycle.

Chapter 4
Planning for the Future
Three Horizons theory and the Innovation Ambitions Matrix.
Three Horizons of Growth
+
Horizon 1 (1-3 years): Incremental improvements to core business. E.g., automating document suites for high-volume work.
Horizon 2 (3-5 years): Step-change improvements, new service lines. E.g., offering practice innovation consulting to clients.
Horizon 3 (5-12 years): Wholly new opportunities to reinvent the business. E.g., law firm product subsidiaries operating under a different business model.
The 70-20-10 resource allocation rule
+
Best practice: allocate roughly 70% of innovation resources to core/sustaining innovation, 20% to adjacent innovation, and 10% to transformational/disruptive innovation. If you focus only on short-term, you fail to protect the organisation long-term. If you focus only on radical innovation, you increase vulnerability now.
Innovation Ambitions Matrix
+
Maps three horizons against "where to play" (existing vs. adjacent vs. new markets) and "how to win" (existing vs. new offerings). Useful for reviewing whether your portfolio of initiatives is skewed too heavily towards one type. Ask: are we only doing H1 work, or do we also have transformational bets in play?

Chapter 5
Building a Portfolio of Initiatives
How to audit your environment and identify the right projects.
Start with a Knowledge & Innovation Audit
+
Survey your constituents across multiple persona groups. Ask about openness to knowledge sharing, familiarity with innovation concepts, awareness of existing tools, client feedback on service delivery, and pain points. This gives you a current-state picture and identifies where to focus.
SWOT Analysis per practice area
+
Approach it practice-by-practice. For each: What are their strengths? What content could be productised? Where are they falling behind? Are there opportunities for client-facing innovation? This reveals the most fruitful areas for your team's efforts.
Listening programs — not a one-off
+
Audits shouldn't be one-and-done. Periodically canvass practices to "take the temperature" — build relationships that generate regular feedback on current and future projects.

Chapter 6
Determining Priorities
How to categorise and prioritise your project portfolio.
The Maker's Matrix
+
Classifies projects along two dimensions: the type of legal activity (Legal Services, Legal Operations, Legal Advice) and the nature of the initiative (Data, Business Models, Thought Leadership). Helps identify what talent each project requires and where it fits in your strategy.
Value vs. Effort framework
+
Evaluate every project by: How many lawyers/practices does it impact? Is the client driving it? Does it align with firm strategy? Could it drive additional business? Then weigh against the resources, time, and effort required. Four categories emerge:
  • Quick wins: High value, low effort — do these first
  • Major projects: High value, high effort — plan carefully
  • Nice-to-haves: Low value, low effort — if time allows
  • Distractions: Low value, high effort — avoid
Business planning by quarter
+
Break your annual plan into quarters. Determine when each project kicks off so you avoid having too many running concurrently. Check resource allocation across the year — without upfront planning, teams burn out and can't execute well on anything.

Chapter 7
Creating and Growing an Innovation Function
Roles, team structures, and hiring for innovation.
Key roles in innovation teams
+
  • CKO / Chief Innovation Officer: 15-20 years experience, strategic leadership, political navigation
  • Director of KM / Innovation: Strategy, recruiting, culture building
  • KM Lawyers: Bridge between practice and innovation
  • Legal Technologists: Coding, SQL, automation, data viz, ML
  • Solutions Architect / Product Manager: For client-facing products
  • Data Analysts: Increasingly critical for dashboards and insights
Hire for curiosity and making, not just legal pedigree
+
The best innovation team members are makers — people who do woodwork, knitting, music production. They share curiosity, resilience, comfort with failure, and creativity. Look for "chaos pilots" who thrive in ambiguity. Don't hire solely based on law school rankings; hire for organisational skills, attention to detail, and genuine interest in how things work.
Diverse teams perform better
+
Diversity of thought means a wider idea space during brainstorming and greater reach of solutions during problem-solving. Research confirms diverse teams are smarter and more likely to process facts carefully. Guard against affinity bias in hiring.

Chapter 8
Supercharging Your Strategy
TNTs, bright spots, and appreciative inquiry — optimistic approaches to change.
Tiny Noticeable Things (TNTs)
+
Small gestures that slip past the brain's resistance to change and create lasting positive impact. A legal technologist demonstrating Word macros that eliminate formatting frustrations had more impact than any major platform demo. Don't underestimate the power of tiny things to improve lives.
Find the Bright Spots
+
Instead of always looking for problems, find where things are already working well and replicate those successes. Example: an associate who invented a numbering system for discovery responses — amplifying that to the whole practice group improved efficiency without buying any new technology.
Appreciative Inquiry (4-D Cycle)
+
A strengths-based approach to change: Discovery (identify what's working well) → Dream (envision the future if best practices were amplified) → Design (construct the path) → Destiny (sustain the change). Particularly useful for rejuvenating jaded team members.
Mental health note: Law is particularly vulnerable to mental health issues. Innovative approaches to reducing repetitive work can have a real impact on lawyer wellbeing. Make sure your team gets the support they need.

Chapter 9
Change Management
The change curve, Kotter's 8 steps, and managing resistance.
Four principles of change
+
  1. Change is registered in the same brain region as pain
  2. The brain forms expectations of new tools — unmet expectations create stress
  3. Managing expectations at the outset is critical
  4. Change is a process with distinct stages (denial → resistance → exploration → acceptance)
Kotter's 8 Steps
+
  1. Increase urgency
  2. Build a guiding team
  3. Develop a vision
  4. Communicate to get buy-in
  5. Empower action
  6. Create short-term wins
  7. Don't give up
  8. Make the change stick
Create a change management roadmap template that your team reviews for each project.
Dimensions of change — assess your project's complexity
+
Evaluate each project on four dimensions: Breadth (how many people affected), Depth (how profound the change), Impact (how many systems affected), Criticality (how vital to the organisation). A project scoring high on multiple dimensions needs a full change team and significant investment.
Change agents vs. saboteurs
+
Change agents: Positive, well-liked, comfortable with change, energetic. Identify them early and recruit them to champion projects.

Saboteurs: Negative, entrenched, find reasons things can't work. Don't fight them head-on — let them save face, praise improvements, play to ego. If their reputation benefits from getting on board, they'll likely stop resisting.

Chapter 10
Communications & Storytelling
The power of narrative, WIIFM, personas, and the Rule of Seven.
WIIFM — "What's In It For Me?"
+
Every communication must answer this from the recipient's perspective. People want to know what they'll lose, how much time it will take to learn, and what the expectations are. Tailor messaging to different persona groups — a partner's priorities differ from an associate's.
The Rule of Seven
+
People need to see or hear a message at least seven times before acting. Use multiple channels: email, intranet, Slack, posters in kitchenettes, TV displays, launch videos, firm podcasts, elevator chats, branded swag. Consistency across channels is key.
See-Feel-Change (not Analyze-Think-Change)
+
We underestimate the power of emotion. Instead of "improve efficiency by using this tool," try "make your client happier" or "spend an extra half hour with your family." Use ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), and logos (logic) together.
Elevator pitches and active listening
+
Develop short, compelling elevator pitches for each solution. Train your team in active listening — maintain eye contact, don't interrupt, ask clarifying questions, summarise what you've heard. This builds trust and uncovers real needs.

Chapter 11
Creative Problem-Solving & Process Improvement
Design thinking, Lean Six Sigma, Kaizen, and root cause analysis.
Design Thinking (IDEO)
+
Five stages: Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test. 79% of companies using it agree it improves ideation. The key insight: don't solve a problem before you properly understand it. Start with business departments, get wins, then move to lawyers, then to clients.
Lean Six Sigma & DMAIC
+
Define → Measure → Analyze → Improve → Control. Focuses on eliminating waste in processes. Most widely applied in legal project management. Seyfarth Shaw became renowned for adopting Lean Six Sigma in 2005. Consider having team members certified.
Kaizen — continuous improvement culture
+
Small, ongoing positive changes reap significant improvements. Everyone at all levels works together proactively. When people feel their opinions matter, they're more engaged and more likely to stay — critical for law firm retention.
Root cause tools: 5 Whys & Fishbone
+
5 Whys: Ask "why" five times to get past symptoms to root causes. Example: Partner says she needs better work tracking → 5 whys reveals the real issue is concern about associate wellbeing and retention.

Fishbone (Ishikawa): Map causes across categories (People, Methods, Equipment, Environment) to understand multi-faceted problems.

Chapter 12
Approaches to Work & Ways of Working
Porter's Five Forces, Blue Ocean Strategy, The Innovator's Dilemma, and Agile.
Porter's Five Forces for legal
+
Apply competitive analysis to legal: Buyer power (clients demanding innovation), Supplier power (lawyers themselves), New entrants (ALSPs, Big Four), Substitutes (technology replacing parts of legal work), Rivalry (competition for shrinking market share). Use this to refine your innovation strategy.
Blue Ocean Strategy
+
Instead of competing in crowded "red oceans," create uncontested market space. Innovation teams are well-positioned to identify blue ocean opportunities — new service lines, products, or approaches that make competition irrelevant. Technology is sometimes involved but isn't a defining feature.
The Innovator's Dilemma (Christensen)
+
Essential reading for legal innovators. Key takeaways:
  • The status quo is not equipped to nurture disruptive innovation
  • Resources won't naturally be allocated to disruptive efforts
  • The market for your disruption isn't your current client base
  • You need a safe space where failure is allowed
  • Capabilities needed for disruption differ from those for core work
Agile and Scrum for legal innovation
+
Agile is a philosophy, not a rigid structure: individuals over processes, working solutions over documentation, collaboration over contracts, responding to change over following a plan. Use sprints and standups for your innovation projects. Organise into smaller cross-functional teams for better focus and engagement.

Chapter 13
Product Management
V2MOM, Business Model Canvas, JTBD, and Build/Partner/Buy.
Why products matter in law
+
Products have innate advantages over services: an expert doesn't need to be available for a client to access expertise. Parts of legal work lend themselves to repackaging at a flat rate. Value lies in the output, not the hours required to deliver it. Firms that recognise this can differentiate and scale.
V2MOM Framework (Salesforce)
+
Vision: What do you want to accomplish?
Values: What's important about it?
Methods: How will you realise the vision?
Obstacles: What might stand in the way?
Measures: How will you know when you've achieved it?
Jobs To Be Done (JTBD)
+
The unit of analysis isn't the customer or the product — it's the core functional job the customer is trying to get done. Formula: "WHEN I [situation], I WANT TO [motivation], SO I CAN [desired outcome]." Thinking about lawyers' and clients' problems from this perspective helps you make more impact.
Build, Partner, or Buy
+
Decision framework for any technology need:
  1. Use existing: Can you combine/reconfigure tools you already have?
  2. Buy: Is there a third-party solution to license?
  3. Partner: Can a vendor develop something with you?
  4. Build: If nothing else works and the value justifies it, build custom.

Chapter 14
Incentives & Adoption
The psychology of adoption, status quo bias, and the adoption lifecycle.
The 9x Effect — why adoption fails
+
Consumers overweight the incumbent product's benefits by a factor of roughly 3x, while developers overweight the new product's benefits by 3x. Combined, the new product must be ~9x better than the old to overcome status quo bias. This explains why many innovations fail despite being objectively superior.
Four types of adoption scenarios
+
  • Easy sells: Limited product + behaviour change — quick adoption
  • Smash hits: Major product improvement, minimal behaviour change — highest success
  • Long hauls: Major changes to both product and behaviour (e.g., DMS) — needs years of work
  • Rapid failures: Major behaviour change, limited product benefit — avoid these
The Adoption Lifecycle (Rogers)
+
Innovators (2.5%) → Early Adopters (13.5%) → Early Majority (34%) → Late Majority (34%) → Laggards (16%). Early adopters are key influencers with extensive networks — their word-of-mouth support is critical. Once a critical mass develops, momentum becomes self-sustaining.
Minimise behaviour change & add autonomy
+
Integrate new solutions into existing workflows rather than asking people to change how they work entirely. Provide signposts showing exactly where in their usual process to access the new solution. Add a degree of autonomy — people adopt faster when they feel they're choosing, not being forced.
KWM case study: King & Wood Mallesons treats innovation as mission-critical. They link innovation to client engagements so lawyers can roll innovation work into client work, reducing the need for extra time. Certifications and formal recognition for innovation success are embedded in the firm's culture.
