Core thesis: Agile business analysis is ordinary business analysis done with an agile mindset — a constant focus on business value, fast feedback, and learning. The goal is to maximise outcome (value delivered) with minimum output, and to decide what to do next using evidence rather than opinion, at every level from strategy to the daily backlog.

Chapter 1
Introduction
Why the Agile Extension exists, what agile business analysis is, and how the book is structured.
Agile is a mindset, not a set of practices
+
Agile ideas began in software but have spread into any area of the business where change happens quickly — so business analysis has to evolve to support those new ways of working. The Extension uses "agile" to mean a way of thinking about work, not a particular toolkit. There is no single approach that fits every context; part of the practitioner's skill is picking the most effective technique for the situation.
Why a Version 2? Linking agile BA to BABOK v3
+
Version 1 came out in 2013; the state of practice moved on. Version 2 (2017) exists mainly to describe the link between business analysis practised with an agile mindset and the BABOK® Guide v3. Its biggest structural addition is a multi-level, rolling planning model built on three horizons — Strategy, Initiative, Delivery — where each horizon gives context and scope to the one below it.
Agile delivery = fast feedback + short decision cycles
+
Agile delivery is framed as a business strategy that creates value through rapid feedback. Planning takes two forms: iterative (prioritise and refine in short cycles to maximise learning) and adaptive (continuously change long-term plans as you learn). Value is delivered incrementally — slice the product small, prioritise by value, ship often. The Extension is approach-agnostic: techniques from both forms can be combined.
Feedback is the organisation's learning engine
+
Organisations get feedback from customers, competitors, partners, investors, SMEs, and regulators. Agile BA uses that feedback to prioritise what delivers most value and eliminate what doesn't. Because delivery always involves uncertainty, delivering value in a minimum viable way, as early as possible, is how you learn what's actually valuable and minimise waste.
What agile BA practitioners actually do
+
Four core activities:
  • Link strategy to initiatives — connect the organisation's goals to the work resourced to achieve them.
  • Discover, interpret, and communicate information to clarify where value can be created.
  • Clarify for whom value is created, who contributes, and who else is impacted.
  • Help stakeholders decide on approaches, priorities, and trade-offs amid constraints, differing opinions, risk, and complexity.
Alongside this: "maximising the amount of work not done" as a deliberate waste-reduction strategy, and fostering an environment of creativity, rapid learning, and experimentation.
Three horizons, three granularities
+
A planning horizon is a view of work at a granularity appropriate to its time frame and feedback loops. Organisations may use different names and spans — the concept of planning at multiple nested horizons is what matters.
  • Strategy: all work across the organisation; which initiatives to fund, how, and how to monitor them. Feedback = are business goals being met?
  • Initiative: one product (internal or customer-facing); its features and how they split into discrete pieces of value. Feedback = customer/user interaction with the product.
  • Delivery: where work happens; teams build pieces from a value-prioritised list using iterative/adaptive and incremental techniques.
How the book is organised
+
Core content: chapters 4–6, the Strategy, Initiative, and Delivery Horizons. Extended content: chapters 2, 3, and 7 (Mindset, Multiple Horizons, Techniques). Appendices: Glossary, a mapping of every BABOK® task to the three horizons, Contributors, and a summary of changes from v1.
Chapter 2
The Agile Mindset
Values from the Agile Manifesto, a shared vocabulary from the BACCM, and seven principles that guide agile business analysis.
One-liner: maximise the outcome (value delivered) with minimum output — "do less and do the right things, right."
What an agile mindset consists of
+
Rooted in human values — respect, courage, collaboration, continuous learning, customer focus, value maximisation. The main aspects:
  • Deliver value rapidly and consistently
  • Collaborate courageously
  • Iterate to learn
  • Simplify to avoid waste
  • Consider context and adapt to realities
  • Reflect on feedback and adapt both product and process
  • Produce the highest-quality products
Mindset vs methodology vs framework
+
A mindset describes attitudes and ideas, not exact actions. The particular combination of techniques, processes, and tools a team picks for its context is its methodology. Branded frameworks (Scrum, Kanban, XP, Lean, SAFe, LeSS, DAD…) are bundles of practices proven in some context — they share respect for people, rapid delivery and feedback, collaboration for shared understanding, and slicing work into small increments of value. But the context where a framework worked once may not match yours: there is no "one size fits all", and many teams mix practices from several frameworks.
Why agile applies to business analysis at all
+
The ideas behind agile predate software and apply to any domain that derives value from creative human work — marketing, law and architecture firms, consultancies, pharma discovery. Business analysis is human-centric, exploratory, and creative, and its results change how organisations run. So it fits.
The Agile Manifesto, re-read for analysis
+
Swap "working software" for "working solution" and the Manifesto becomes a philosophy of analysis:
  • "We are uncovering better ways… by doing it" — the most important line: agile is empirical. You learn what works by trying, not theorising.
  • Individuals and interactions — BA is human-centric; solutions change how people work, so put people at the centre.
  • Working solutions over comprehensive documentation — produce something, show it, get feedback. Documentation has value only when written for its actual purpose.
  • Customer collaboration — understanding of the need is refined by showing increments and analysing responses; collaboration also surfaces new needs.
  • Responding to change over following a plan — agile does plan (e.g. a product roadmap), but success is measured by how well the solution satisfies the need, not plan conformance.

Chapter 3
Analysis at Multiple Horizons
Why one plan isn't enough: nested planning horizons, how feedback travels between them, and the economics of predictive, iterative, and adaptive planning.
Sense-and-respond locally, look ahead globally
+
In fast-changing environments organisations must respond to local opportunities and problems without involving the whole organisation, while still watching for emerging threats and opportunities. Horizons frame the shift in focus between long-term strategic needs and the immediate needs of a customer. Several practitioners may each focus on one horizon, or one person may span several — either way, constant communication across horizons is essential.
Same mindset and principles at every horizon — applied differently
+
The agile mindset, the seven principles, and many of the same practices apply at all three horizons. What changes is who is involved, the time span, the level of detail, and how techniques are applied. Appendix B maps every BABOK® task to each horizon. Ongoing collaboration across horizons gives decision makers more current, accurate information — and better outcomes.

Predictive, iterative, and adaptive planning

Three root forms of planning. Real organisations combine them; the Extension argues adaptive planning is central to agile BA.

What it is
Both long- and short-term planning, where long-term plans are expected to change and are easy to change. Short-term plans inform the long-term ones. Sometimes called rolling wave planning: every plan is a hypothesis to be proven, adjusted in real time as feedback and lessons arrive. Higher horizons are longer and more abstract, so there is less detail to change and less resistance to changing it.
Economic assumption
Cost of change rises as work progresses, and the opportunity cost of suppressing feedback is high. Learning comes from ongoing analysis and feedback; implementation is continuous and creates learning for future work.
Planning horizon
Continuous and nested (fractal): strategic planning gives vision and scope to initiatives, while information from delivered work feeds back into strategic thinking.
Benefits
The organisation can rapidly "sense and respond" to new information while keeping sight of long-term goals, so the real cost of change falls. Creates a context that supports an agile mindset.
Issues
Significant complexity in constantly updating plans; demands continuous collaboration and feedback. Can look inefficient. Without transparency, trust, and collaboration it produces confusion, mistakes, and wasted effort.
What it is
Used when rapid change and uncertainty make long-term planning ineffective: the next step is based on the latest learning. Each hypothesis is tested before planning the next move, because stakeholders are learning what they need at the same time as planning happens. Plans are viable only for the immediate future — perhaps the next one to four weeks.
Economic assumption
Cost of change is flat; it does not grow as work progresses. High opportunity cost if learning is suppressed. Learning is best facilitated by quickly building and soliciting feedback.
Planning horizon
A series of short, detailed horizons. A plan exists only for the current iteration and takes advantage of the latest feedback.
Benefits
Highly responsive to learning. Very efficient — there is no long plan to maintain. Practitioners spot opportunities and threats as they emerge through constant testing of assumptions.
Issues
Doesn't scale well and can feel ad hoc. Lack of a longer-range objective can make planning random. In large or complex contexts there may be insufficient time to analyse ideas that touch many parts of the solution, leading to rework.
What it is
Detailed analysis and planning up front, then execution. Driven by the cost-of-change curve: the later a mistake is found, the more it costs to fix, so spend more time analysing early to minimise gaps and defects. Scope and risk are predicted at the start; the planning horizon is the whole project; deviations are treated as risks to be corrected.
Economic assumption
Cost of change grows exponentially. Low opportunity cost from suppressed learning because scope can be defined early. Learning is best done through up-front analysis.
Planning horizon
A single long, detailed horizon spanning the duration of the project. All work is measured as conformance to the plan.
Benefits
Well understood by any organisation. Efficiency comes from certainty in executing an existing plan. A well-prepared planner can identify risks and build contingencies.
Issues
Slow to respond to learning and may actively suppress it. An unforeseen event can disrupt the plan — or worse, an opportunity can't be taken because changing the plan is impractical.
Chapter 4 · Core content
Strategy Horizon
Purpose: inform decisions regarding the organisation's business goals. The widest scope, the longest look-ahead, and the least detail.
Value here = understanding and achieving business goals
+
Goals change; consumer tastes shift; competitors disrupt; regulations move. The Strategy Horizon continually reassesses the priority of goals and evaluates emerging opportunities, so the organisation can redeploy resources as fast as it learns. The challenge: information here is often overwhelming, obscure, uncertain, even contradictory — and even good information is hard to communicate. The risk is decisions made on outdated or wrong data when dependable data exists somewhere in the organisation.
Observe change across a broad horizon
+
Analysis scope extends beyond any single initiative. Practitioners make sense of change in five areas:
  • Customer expectations — including shifts invisible to initiative teams, via research and analytics.
  • The outside environment — competitors, economic and social conditions, emerging technology.
  • Inside the organisation — constant internal change forces redirection of resources and new goals.
  • Learning from the front line — a major economic driver for agile is learning quickly which initiatives to stop funding (value delivered, or never going to be) and which to boost.
  • Threats and opportunities — start an initiative to counter or exploit; change resourcing, scope or success criteria; cancel what's become less relevant.
Timeliness matters: observations must be acted on fast enough to still be relevant.
Level of detail: stop before the initiative specifics
+
Strategy-level analysis understands how organisational goals map to initiative goals, then stops — the people inside each initiative are better placed to analyse its details. The Strategy practitioner identifies a potential need and gives the Initiative team just enough to understand it and develop solutions. Focus stays on risks, changing circumstances, and new needs that might re-prioritise decisions across initiatives. There is no clean hand-off line between horizons.
Reduce complexity so decisions can be made
+
The systems being analysed (initiative performance, competitor moves, shifting inputs) are unstable and understanding is always incomplete. Practitioners break systems into parts and use models to reduce large volumes of confusing data into enough information for a defensible and testable decision.
Time frame: a rolling 3 months to many years
+
Much of the analysis is speculation about the future. The further out you look, the more plans become aspirational hypotheses rather than facts. The guiding question: "as an organisation, what do we need to learn to prove or disprove our long-term hypothesis?" Practitioners constantly switch between micro-detail of a potential change and the broad view of its impact on the whole.
Feedback & learning: the five "should we start?" questions
+
Decision makers often delay launching initiatives because they can't learn fast enough to feel confident. Agile BA provides just enough information to decide, by answering:
  • Is there a need to satisfy?
  • Is the need aligned with our strategic objectives?
  • Is it worth satisfying?
  • Do we believe we have the right team for a good-enough solution?
  • Can we measure the initiative's success?
Once started, further analysis moves to the Initiative Horizon. Because many practitioners and decision makers work independently in different domains (an accountant watching cash flow, a marketer watching customer behaviour), the BA's role is to facilitate feedback and learning between them.

Chapter 5 · Core content
Initiative Horizon
Purpose: inform decisions regarding solution options, features, priorities, and lifespan. The goal: deliver a solution that minimises output and maximises outcome.
The six questions this horizon answers: What solution options satisfy the need? · Which gives maximum outcome for minimum output within constraints? · What are the components (features) of the preferred option? · What should be delivered now, next, and in future? · Has enough value been delivered to satisfy the need? · Should the solution continue, change, or be cancelled?
What flows in from Strategy
+
Three things, communicated continuously: the shared understanding of the need, why the need exists, and the determination that it's worth satisfying given current understanding of benefits, risks, and constraints. The Initiative Horizon is largely about how much and what kind of analysis is needed to support a series of decisions, from defining the need to identifying the solution aspects that will satisfy it.
Identify solution options
+
Most solutions are complex enough to have multiple viable options, each with multiple components (features). To assess options the team needs shared understanding of the need and clear, measurable objectives. Practitioners consider: the shared understanding of the need, assumptions about viability, risks introduced, a very broad description of each option, and constraints that rule options out. Generating options also surfaces previously unknown information.
Recommend an option (same precision for every option)
+
Recommend the option with maximum outcome for minimum output within constraints; the accountable decision maker decides whether to proceed. Compare options at the same level of precision, with just enough information for an accurate decision. Practitioners validate assumptions, identify ways to assess practicality, consider impacts on people, processes, tools, systems, and vendors, assess projected impact on the need, and estimate cost in time, money or other resources. Experimentation is encouraged — just enough to pick an option.
Identify components — this list becomes the backlog
+
With stakeholders, identify the parts of the solution that deliver the outcome (in agile contexts: features). Each is evaluated against outcome, cost, impact, and constraints at a moderate, consistent level of precision. The resulting list is what many agile contexts call the backlog. This is iterative: items are added and removed as understanding of each component's value emerges.
Prioritise and sequence (refining the backlog)
+
Assess priority and sequence on:
  • impact of each component on the overall outcome
  • cost (actual or in team capacity)
  • current state of the initiative
  • stakeholder feedback
  • current performance toward the outcome
  • current understanding of constraints and risks
Re-done many times: whenever new information arrives, and at release planning, roadmap updates, iteration planning, or scheduled prioritisation sessions.
Is the need satisfied yet? Ask after every component
+
Judged by whether outputs delivered meet the desired outcome — not by whether all planned components are done. Practitioners consider it every time a component ships and feedback returns, which cuts the risk of producing more output than needed. Once the outcome is achieved, effort shifts to the next need. If not, consider continue / change / cancel.
Ongoing viability: continue, change, or cancel
+
Solutions are continually assessed against identified measures of success and feedback from both other horizons. If the solution isn't delivering enough value, or new information changes things, practitioners can change it (add/remove components, re-sequence) or cancel the initiative so resources can be redeployed. Viability is judged on:
  • impact of the current solution on the outcomes
  • identified measures of success
  • anticipated work to finish the component list
  • ongoing assessment of constraints and risks
  • a clarified understanding of the need from Delivery
  • a clarified understanding of the need's strategic relevance from Strategy
Time frame & the roadmap questions
+
Mid-term: as a general reference, the upcoming one to three months, rolling forward. Three driving questions form the basis of a solution roadmap: What outcomes are we driving now? What will we address next? What will we address in the future?
Feedback in both directions
+
From Delivery, feedback tells you whether the option and components are still appropriate, whether they're sequenced correctly, and whether every component actually needs delivering. To Strategy, the Initiative Horizon reports changes that may affect other initiatives — a cancellation frees resources; a scope change may need more. To Delivery, it sends priority and sequencing.

Chapter 6 · Core content
Delivery Horizon
Purpose: inform decisions regarding the delivery of the solution. Where the work happens: slicing, refining, and shipping increments from a prioritised backlog.
What analysis looks like here
+
Focus on the specific slice of the solution being implemented now. Practitioners ensure shared understanding of the need, identify and prioritise a backlog of actions, and set up a way to assess outcomes — spending the least effort needed to make informed decisions. Requirements are mapped clearly to business goals. User stories are sliced and elaborated just-in-time into implementable units of value; feedback from testing and customers creates, alters, re-prioritises, and removes backlog items.
When is a user story "ready"?
+
A story is well-written when it:
  • meets the INVEST criteria (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Sized appropriately, Testable)
  • has a well-constructed narrative
  • has clear, concise, precise acceptance criteria
  • is accepted as a desirable unit of implementation
  • is an achievable unit of development
  • is clearly prioritised in the backlog
A story only needs to be ready when it's about to go into development. Refining too early causes rework and waste because conditions change.
Maintaining the backlog: two jobs
+
(1) Priority sequencing — with the product owner, rapid delivery of business value as the central consideration; backlog order must reflect the PO's priority. (2) Enough ready items for near-term development — create features, decompose into stories, refine into well-written stories. Feedback from delivered work changes the backlog continuously, formally in refinement meetings or informally as needed.
Supporting successful delivery
+
Clear analysis-related roadblocks and apply learning so they don't recur: handle story sequencing and dependencies, coordinate with external teams and stakeholders, and answer clarifying questions on items currently mid-implementation.
Making sure learning happens — process and product
+
Process learning: what should be changed, kept, or stopped in the next cycle? Retrospectives are the usual forum. Product learning: did the last increment deliver the value expected? Answers may change the nature or priority of near-term stories. Anything too broad, large, or far-off is passed up as feedback to the Initiative or Strategy Horizons. Better outcomes can be quantitative or qualitative.
Keep focus on vision, customer, and value
+
Apply BA techniques and the seven principles to stay anchored to the value being sought and the product vision. Constant communication and a shared understanding of need and outcomes help every stakeholder avoid waste and deliver value quickly.
Time frame & feedback cadence
+
Day-to-day delivery planning over roughly one to four weeks, up to six to eight, rolling forward. Feedback is rapid and in small chunks; each story is a small increment, but significant feedback usually needs a collection of stories across several increments. Feedback arrives through structured events (reviews, retrospectives) and informally through daily work. Avoid Waste and Analyze to Determine What is Valuable guide the refinement decisions it drives.

Appendix B
BABOK® Tasks × Horizons
How each BABOK® Guide knowledge area is applied with an agile mindset at the three horizons — condensed from the full task-by-task mapping. The mapping is guidance, not a rule: any task can be applied creatively at any horizon.
3. BA Planning & Monitoring StrategyDecide that agile is the approach; keep planning lightweight. Establish an adaptive portfolio-management and decision framework for the whole organisation. Stakeholder engagement spans all initiatives. InitiativeSelect the specific agile approach (Scrum, Kanban…) and decide how the backlog will be refined and prioritised. Measures focus on the ability to learn and adapt. DeliveryPlan the fastest, most accurate way to decompose features to stories for the immediate work; define refinement rhythm, information radiators, and how change is communicated inside the team.
4. Elicitation & Collaboration StrategyOngoing, broad, and lightweight — elicit and communicate about goals, markets, and risks with leaders across initiatives. InitiativeElicit with the product owner and cross-functional team to understand options, features, and priorities; confirm understanding through examples and collaboration. DeliveryContinuous, conversational elicitation around stories (the "conversation" in the 3Cs); collaboration happens daily and in reviews and retrospectives.
5. Requirements Life Cycle Management StrategyTrace initiatives to organisational goals; prioritise goals and initiatives rather than detailed requirements. Approval = empowering initiatives by communicating evolving assumptions, constraints, and goals. InitiativeTrace features to the goals and metrics set at Strategy. Prioritisation is about which features to deliver, not necessarily their order. Changes are assessed as a lightweight part of backlog refinement; approval happens when the lead selects a requirement for delivery. DeliveryTrace stories to features and outcomes. Monitor stories for staleness; revise, re-prioritise, or remove as needs change. Prioritisation changes frequently as learning arrives. Work is approved by someone other than its creator, ideally with a view of the value sought.
6. Strategy Analysis StrategyCurrent and future state are continuously re-analysed through collaboration, feedback, and learning; multiple potential future states across time horizons are continually defined and redefined. InitiativeAt the start, analyse the current state at a high level to understand the whole, then find the most valuable parts to change. Define the future state as the outcome the initiative pursues. DeliveryWith the product owner, examine the specific portion of the current solution immediately affected; scope stays tightly focused on the near term.
7. Requirements Analysis & Design Definition StrategyRequirements may be ambiguous and expressed as organisational goals; make sure feedback frameworks exist to keep alignment as goals evolve. InitiativeSpecify and model at the level of solution options and features; compare options at consistent precision; recommend the option that maximises outcome for minimum output. DeliveryJust-in-time, just-enough: elaborate stories into acceptance criteria and lightweight models for the upcoming iteration only.
8. Solution Evaluation StrategyEvaluate whether initiatives are delivering against business goals; use that learning to fund, re-scope, or stop initiatives. InitiativeAfter every delivered component, ask whether the need is satisfied; assess ongoing viability and decide to continue, change, or cancel. DeliveryEvaluate each increment's value through reviews and feedback; feed the result back into the backlog and, where it's bigger than the team, up to the other horizons.
